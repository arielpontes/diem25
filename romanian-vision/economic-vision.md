# We envision a Romanian economy that works for the many who create its wealth — not for the foreign shareholders who extract it.

*Like every DiEM25 Romania vision document, this one is concrete about direction and deliberately silent on fine detail. Every policy we name already works somewhere in Europe, and we cite the laws so you can verify us. The fully specified, costed Romanian versions will be drafted democratically by our members — and, when the time comes, by candidates who put them before voters.*

Thirty-five years ago, Romanians were promised that the market would bring them the living standards of the West. What arrived instead was the West's shopping list. Our banks are Austrian and French. Our energy champion is Austrian. Our supermarkets — Mega Image, Lidl, Kaufland, Carrefour, Auchan — answer to shareholders in Amsterdam, in the German town of Neckarsulm, in Paris. Every year, billions in profits made from Romanian work and Romanian consumption leave the country, often barely taxed, while the Romanian shopkeeper, farmer and freelancer carries the state on their back.

Let us be clear about who our quarrel is with. It is not with the Dutch, German or French people. Their workers fought for — and won — short working weeks, strong unions, seats on company boards and taxes on great fortunes. Our quarrel is with corporations that enjoy those workers' victories at home while treating Romania as a low-wage, low-tax extraction zone. We do not want to punish the West. We want what its working people already have.

Our economic vision is one where Romania is:

- **Fair,** taxing accumulated wealth and corporate extraction instead of squeezing work, consumption and small business.
- **Democratic,** giving the people who work in a company a real voice — and a real share — in how it is run.
- **Free from need,** moving toward an economy where a basic dividend from society's collective wealth belongs to everyone.
- **Rich in time,** because the point of prosperity is life, and Romanians already work among the longest hours in the European Union.

## What we propose

- **Make multinationals pay what they owe.** Rigorously enforce the EU minimum corporate tax of 15% on large multinationals (Council Directive (EU) 2022/2523) and the public country-by-country reporting of their profits (Directive (EU) 2021/2101), and give ANAF the mandate and resources to audit the transfer-pricing schemes through which retail chains and banks move Romanian profits abroad. Where crises produce windfall profits, tax them — as the EU itself did with the solidarity contribution on energy companies (Council Regulation (EU) 2022/1854) and as Spain did with its levies on banks and energy giants.
- **Tax turnover where profits vanish.** Profit taxes fail when the profits are moved to Amsterdam or Luxembourg before the taxman ever sees them — but turnover cannot be moved. Hungary and Poland both levy progressive taxes on the turnover of large retail chains, taxes that in practice fall on the foreign-owned giants while sparing small shops, and the Court of Justice of the EU has upheld their legality (*Tesco-Global*, C-323/18; *Commission v Poland*, C-562/19 P). Let there be no confusion: DiEM25 fights Orbán's authoritarianism everywhere and always. But Romanian democrats should not leave a lawful, court-tested instrument on the table just because an autocrat used it first.
- **Lower the burden on small business and on work.** Every leu recovered from corporate tax avoidance should fund lower and simpler taxation for small businesses, cooperatives and labour. A neighbourhood bakery should never face a heavier effective burden than a multinational's Romanian subsidiary.
- **Tax wealth more, work less.** Romania taxes salaries hard and great fortunes almost not at all. We propose a solidarity tax on large fortunes on the model of Spain's Ley 38/2022, and progressive taxation of large inheritances as practised in France and Germany. On the other side of the ledger: a legal path toward shorter working time, following France's 35-hour week (the Aubry laws of 1998–2000) and Belgium's 2022 labour deal that gave workers the right to a four-day week.
- **Tax the ultra-rich — in Romania and in Europe.** Romania is often imagined as a country too poor to have anyone worth taxing. The numbers say otherwise: Romania has six billionaires on the Forbes world list, and the country's 300 largest fortunes are worth on the order of €25 billion, dozens of them above €100 million — in the member state with the EU's highest in-work poverty. Romania does not lack oligarchs; it lacks the will to tax them. That begins at home, with the solidarity tax on large fortunes proposed above. But wealth hops borders faster than any national law, which is why we also militate, within DiEM25, for a European version of the "Zucman tax": a 2% minimum tax on fortunes above €100 million, adopted by France's National Assembly in 2025 with the support of roughly three-quarters of the French public before the Senate blocked it, and studied at the G20 as a global minimum tax on billionaires. The two levels protect each other: without a European floor, the ultra-rich blackmail every country with the threat of leaving; without national taxes, the floor remains a promise on paper.
- **A European Basic Dividend.** We campaign, at the EU level and as part of DiEM25, for the movement's proposal that a share of large corporations' equity flow into a public depository, paying out a basic dividend to every resident — a return on the collective wealth that all of society, from workers to public infrastructure, helps create. A basic income may sound unrealistic for a country as poor as Romania — but the dividend is not paid from the Romanian budget. It is paid from Europe-wide capital returns, and it is designed to flow from where capital accumulates to where it does not — which is exactly why Romania should be its loudest champion.
- **Economic democracy.** Employees of large companies should elect representatives to their boards, as German workers have done for decades under the Mitbestimmungsgesetz of 1976, with works councils on the model of Germany's Betriebsverfassungsgesetz. When a firm is sold or fails, its workers should have the right to be informed and to bid to buy it themselves, as under France's Hamon Law (Loi 2014-856) and Italy's Marcora Law (Legge 49/1985), which has financed worker buyouts of failing companies for forty years. Cooperatives — enterprises run on one member, one share, one vote — deserve a dedicated legal framework and public financing.

None of this is a Romanian experiment. It is the standard equipment of the European economies Romanians admire. We simply refuse to accept that it stops at our border.
