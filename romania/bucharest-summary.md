# The DiEM25 vision for Bucharest

*Participatory democracy. Ad regulation. Gambling regulation. Community spaces. Urban mobility. Affordable housing and energy.*

Bucharest is a rich city that treats its residents poorly. Its drivers lose more time in traffic than those of almost any city in Europe; the EU's top court has condemned the capital's air pollution as "systematically and persistently" illegal; its residents have roughly a third of the green space per inhabitant that Romanian law has promised them since 2013; and its city hall has built no social housing since 2014. Not for lack of power or money: the levers exist, and they have gone unused — or been used for someone else's benefit.

This is the direction DiEM25 organizes around in Bucharest: a city run as a commons, inspired by policies that already work in other European cities and that sit almost entirely within the powers the city holds today. Our members are now turning this direction into concrete proposals — democratically, and grounded in research. Here is where we start.

## Participatory democracy: a city governed with its residents

Every scandal in this city is also an opacity scandal: lakeside contracts nobody could read, municipal tenant lists nobody could see, a green-space register nobody kept. And between scandals, residents hold power for one day every four years, and none in between.

We are inspired by the cities that govern with their residents: Paris, which puts around 5% of its investment budget — on the order of €100 million a year — [directly in residents' hands](https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2014/oct/08/parisians-have-say-city-first-20m-participatory-budget), and which in 2021 created a [permanent citizens' assembly of one hundred residents drawn by lot](https://www.mehr-demokratie.de/en/mehr-wissen/citizens-assemblies/news/details-view/permanent-citizens-assembly-in-paris/); [Cluj-Napoca](https://stirileprotv.ro/stiri/social/bugetul-participativ-cluj-este-primul-orac-cu-proiecte-de-investic-ii-alese-de-comunitate.html), which has run participatory budgeting since 2017, so the precedent is not just European but Romanian; and Barcelona, which built [Decidim](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/open-source-observatory-osor/news/participatory-democracy), the open-source participation platform now used by cities across Europe — because the infrastructure of democracy should be owned by no one. And underneath it all, a glass city hall: every contract, concession, subsidy and line of spending of the municipality and its companies published as open, searchable data.

## Ad regulation: the view is not for sale

Bucharest's visual space has been sold off surface by surface: facades wrapped in mesh banners, windows covered over, ads in parks and on trees — much of it in defiance of a national advertising law the city has simply never enforced. And the most aggressive buyers of the view are precisely the industries that profit from harming people: the ones that nudge residents to gamble and to eat what makes them sick, keep the profits, and leave the public to pay the costs.

We are inspired by the cities that took their walls and skylines back — and drew a line at ads that push harm: São Paulo, which [removed some 15,000 billboards under its "Clean City" law](https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2015/aug/11/can-cities-kick-ads-ban-urban-billboards) — [the predicted economic collapse never came, and 70% of residents found the ban beneficial](https://newdream.org/blog/sao-paolo-ad-ban); Paris, which [caps the size and number of billboards](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2023/03/06/french-cities-are-banning-billboards) and keeps digital advertising screens off its streets; Italy, which has [banned virtually all gambling advertising since 2018](https://www.thelocal.it/20180705/italy-ban-gambling-adverts); and London, which [banned junk-food ads across its transport network](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2022/feb/17/tfl-junk-food-ad-ban-has-helped-londoners-shop-more-healthily-study) and measured households buying roughly 1,000 fewer calories of unhealthy food a week. Bucharest's first step costs nothing: enforce the advertising law Romania already has.

## Gambling regulation: the power to say no is now local

Romania has become a country of păcănele: more than [45,000 licensed slot machines](https://stirileprotv.ro/stiri/politic/romania-fara-pacanele-si-sali-de-jocuri-de-noroc-in-ce-judete-se-pregatesc-masuri-pentru-interzicerea-lor-dupa-noua-lege.html) blink at residents from halls on every commercial street, extracting their profits from the people who can least afford to lose, while the costs — addiction, debt, broken families — are paid by everyone. But the tide has turned. A [2024 national law banned slot-machine halls from every locality under 15,000 inhabitants](https://www.digi24.ro/stiri/jocurile-de-noroc-sunt-interzise-in-localitatile-mici-klaus-iohannis-a-promulgat-legea-2763561), and since February 2026 [every local council in Romania has the power to ban gambling from its territory outright, to restrict it to designated zones, and to tax it](https://startupcafe.ro/consilii-locale-interzicere-sali-jocuri-noroc-localitate-zone-descarca-oug-7-2026-publicata-monitorul-oficial-95333). [Every city and commune in Iași county has already used that power](https://www.stiridecluj.ro/national/un-judet-din-romania-a-interzis-pacanelele-in-toate-localitatile-sale-cluj-napoca-mai-asteapta-este-un-prim-pas-spre-vindecare), [Dej has done the same](https://www.stiridecluj.ro/social/in-dej-s-a-putut-consiliul-local-a-interzis-jocurile-de-noroc-in-locatii-fizice-urmeaza-cluj-napoca), and [cities from Brăila to Buzău are following](https://stirileprotv.ro/stiri/politic/romania-fara-pacanele-si-sali-de-jocuri-de-noroc-in-ce-judete-se-pregatesc-masuri-pentru-interzicerea-lor-dupa-noua-lege.html). In Bucharest, [Sector 6 has voted for a ban — but the decision that counts belongs to the General Council](https://www.digi24.ro/stiri/actualitate/politica/consiliul-local-sector-6-a-aprobat-proiectul-care-interzice-salile-de-jocuri-de-noroc-decizia-finala-apartine-primariei-capitalei-3704635).

We are also inspired by the countries that treated gambling as the public-health problem it is: the United Kingdom, which [cut the maximum stake on fixed-odds betting terminals from £100 to £2](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-46205812); and Norway, which [removed every slot machine in the country in 2007](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.672471/full) and later reintroduced a far smaller number of state-run terminals with mandatory loss limits — calls to its national gambling helpline fell by roughly 70%. Bucharest does not need to invent anything: the legal power now sits with its own General Council, and half the country is already using it.

## Community spaces: parks, lakes and places to gather

Whenever the city holds something beautiful — a park, a lakeshore — someone has been allowed to fence it, burn it or rent it out. Twelve hectares of IOR Park were taken from the public through a restitution now challenged in court as abusive, and its trees have burned in dozens of fires for which no one has been charged; investigative journalists have counted 44 contracts for lakeside terraces, the longest running to 2039, many built without permits inside a listed park. Yet Văcărești — 186 hectares of self-made wilderness that a civic campaign turned into a protected urban nature park — proves Bucharest wants wild places and knows how to defend them. And beyond nature, the city offers its residents almost nowhere to simply be together: the default meeting place has become the mall, and the spaces where a neighborhood could gather, organize and know itself were never built — or were rented away.

We are inspired by the countries that wrote into law that the shore belongs to everyone: [Brazil](https://www.enca.com/lifestyle/bid-privatize-brazil-beaches-sparks-outcry), where every beach is a public good with free access guaranteed, and [Spain](https://www.spanishpropertyinsight.com/2013/01/11/spains-ley-de-costas-coastal-law-explained/), whose coastal law makes the seashore public domain. Bucharest's parks and lakes deserve the same principle: commerce welcome as small, competitively tendered, revocable kiosks that serve the people using the space — never as an enclosure that prices them out or a gate around the water.

And we are inspired by the cities that hand shared spaces to the communities that bring them alive: Bologna, whose regulation on the urban commons lets residents [sign "collaboration pacts" with the city](https://www.blog.urbact.eu/2016/04/how-a-regulation-turned-bolognas-civic-pride-into-action/) to care for and regenerate shared spaces — more than 130 agreements since 2014, a model adopted by some 60 other Italian municipalities; Barcelona, whose [Citizen Assets programme](https://urbact.eu/articles/barcelona-presents-citizen-assets-programme-community-use-and-management) transfers municipal buildings to the management of neighborhood non-profits; and Lisbon, whose [BIP/ZIP micro-grants](https://urbact.eu/articles/bipzip-strategy-empowering-local-communities-priority-districts-lisbon) have funded some 300 community projects across the city's 67 priority neighborhoods since 2011. A neighborhood that gathers is not only happier — it is the soil participatory democracy grows in.

## Urban mobility: a city you can cross without a car

Bucharest's congestion is among the worst in Europe, and the alternatives have been starved: fares already cover only a small fraction of what public transport costs — the rest is public subsidy — while of the 565 kilometers of bicycle routes the city has promised itself by 2035, under 17 genuinely usable kilometers exist.

We are inspired by the cities that treated getting around as a basic need and made the alternative irresistible: [Tallinn](https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2016/oct/11/tallinn-experiment-estonia-public-transport-free-cities), where public transport has been free for residents since 2013 and the city says the arithmetic paid for itself; [Luxembourg](https://www.euronews.com/2023/03/22/the-worlds-richest-country-made-public-transport-free-heres-what-happened-next), fare-free nationwide since 2020; [Montpellier](https://www.euronews.com/green/2024/05/15/free-public-transport-in-montpellier-has-led-to-20-per-cent-more-journeys), fare-free for residents since 2023, with ridership up by nearly a quarter within months; and Paris, whose sustained investment in cycling meant that by 2024 [more trips inside the city were made by bicycle than by car](https://www.euronews.com/2024/04/12/cycling-is-now-more-popular-than-driving-in-the-centre-of-paris-study-finds).

## Affordable housing and energy: a home you can keep warm — and cool

Romania holds two European records that explain each other: the EU's highest homeownership rate and its highest overcrowding rate — and almost no social housing beneath them. Bucharest's city hall administers a stock it has not added to since 2014, and in 2025 it allocated exactly three social dwellings. Meanwhile the city runs one of the largest district-heating networks in the world and loses roughly a third of the heat in it, in a country where one person in ten cannot keep their home adequately warm — and as the climate crisis lengthens Bucharest's heat waves, summer electricity bills are joining winter heating bills among the costs residents fear.

We are inspired by [Vienna](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2024/jan/10/the-social-housing-secret-how-vienna-became-the-worlds-most-livable-city), where around 60% of residents live in municipal or limited-profit housing with cost-based rents, and where major rezonings are required to deliver subsidized homes — so that land-value windfalls pay for public housing; by Finland, whose [Housing First policy](https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2019/jun/03/its-a-miracle-helsinkis-radical-solution-to-homelessness) gives homeless people a permanent home first and support second, and has cut long-term homelessness by two-thirds while saving public money; by Denmark, whose municipalities have [planned their heating decades ahead since 1979](https://danskfjernvarme.dk/english/english/the-danish-model) — the foundation of [Copenhagen's district heating](https://www.c40.org/case-studies/cities100-copenhagen-carbon-neutral-district-heating/), which reaches nearly every building and is close to carbon-neutral; and by Vienna again, whose public utility [sells residents shares in the solar plants it builds](https://www.themayor.eu/en/a/view/vienna-sells-shares-of-new-solar-park-to-citizens-10112) — the first sold out in 26 hours — so that even people who rent and own no roof can own a piece of the energy transition; and by France, which is [requiring every large car park to be covered with solar canopies](https://www.thelocal.fr/20221110/france-set-to-make-solar-panels-compulsory-in-all-large-car-parks), turning seas of asphalt into clean, cheap power.

---

Little of this needs new money so much as the end of old bleeding: Bucharest already pays, every year, for heat lost into the ground, rents never collected, fines never enforced and land given away. And almost all of it lies within the city's own powers; where it does not, we say so and name who must act. This vision is also an invitation: the research, the local knowledge and the democratic drafting of concrete proposals come next, and they are open to everyone who wants Bucharest to belong to the people who live in it. Join us in writing them.
