# Bucharest belongs to the people who live in it — not to the billboard owners, the betting industry and the concession-holders who treat it as a revenue stream.

*Like every DiEM25 Romania vision document, this one is concrete about direction and deliberately silent on fine detail. Every policy we name already works in another city or country — usually in Europe — and we cite the laws so you can verify us. Because this document is about a city, we add one more discipline: everything we propose sits within powers the General Mayor and the General Council already hold. The fully specified, costed versions will be drafted democratically by our members — and, when the time comes, by candidates who put them before voters.*

Bucharest is a rich city that treats its residents poorly. Its drivers lost 171 hours to rush-hour congestion in 2025 — the fifth-worst in Europe by TomTom's index. The Court of Justice of the EU condemned Romania in 2020 (Case C-638/18) for "systematically and persistently" illegal air pollution in the capital. Independent estimates put its green space at around 9–10 square meters per inhabitant, against the 26 the law has required since the end of 2013 (OUG 114/2007). The General City Hall has built no social housing since 2014 and allocated exactly three social dwellings in 2025. Roughly a third of the heat its residents pay for is lost in the pipes before it ever reaches a radiator. And whenever the city does hold something valuable — a park, a lakeshore, a wall, a window — someone has been allowed to fence it, cover it or rent it out.

None of this is because the city government lacks power. Bucharest's two-tier structure — a General Mayor and six sector mayors — is dysfunctional by design, but the levers the General Council does hold — the General Urbanism Plan, the outdoor advertising regulation, transport fares, public housing, the parks and lakes administration — have gone unused, or been used for someone else's benefit. Our Bucharest will be:

- **Ours to look at,** a city whose visual space is a commons, not an inventory of advertising surfaces for sale.
- **Ours to walk into,** with parks, lakeshores and urban wilderness that no one can fence off, burn down or turn into a terrace with a minimum spend.
- **Ours to move through,** with public transport free at the point of use and streets where a bicycle is a serious way to get to work.
- **Ours to live in,** with a public housing stock worthy of a European capital, allocated by need and never by connections — and homes that stay warm all winter.
- **Ours to govern,** with real budgets and real decisions in residents' hands every day of the year — not only a ballot every four years.

## What we propose

### Take back the city's visual space

- **Enforce the advertising law Romania already has.** Law 185/2013 prohibits covering the glazed surfaces — the windows — of buildings with advertising (art. 15(2)); prohibits ads in parks, on trees and on public buildings (art. 15(1)); allows the mesh banners that swallow whole residential facades only where a local regulation explicitly permits them (art. 38); and empowers the General Mayor to demolish non-conforming panels administratively, without going to court (art. 5, art. 57). Yet Bucharest's advertising regulation dates from 2011 and predates the law itself. Step one costs nothing: adopt the regulation, then use the demolition power.
- **Cap the size and the density of ads.** France lets every municipality adopt a *Règlement Local de Publicité* (Code de l'environnement, art. L581-14) restricting the size, density and illumination of outdoor advertising below national ceilings — the national default for a billboard is now 10.5 m², and Paris's 2023 regulation cut the number of panels further while keeping digital advertising screens banned. Grenoble let its street-advertising contract expire in 2014 and dismantled 326 advertising installations, planting trees in their place. São Paulo, a city of 12 million, removed some 15,000 billboards under its Cidade Limpa law (Lei 14.223/2006) — the predicted economic collapse never came, and approval ran at 70%. Bucharest's new regulation should define restricted zones covering most of the city, with hard limits on panel size and per-street density.
- **Not one gambling ad on anything the city owns.** Romania has some 15,000 street betting agencies, and Romanians spent about €2.5 billion in a single year on online gambling alone; national law today imposes little more than a 35 m² size cap on gambling billboards (OUG 82/2023, approved by Law 107/2024). A city cannot ban gambling advertising citywide — that is national competence — but it fully controls its own property: ban gambling ads from every building, panel, vehicle and contract of the municipality and its companies, as Bristol and Sheffield have banned them from council-owned sites. And militate nationally for what already exists across Europe: Italy has banned virtually all gambling advertising since 2018 (*Decreto Dignità*, DL 87/2018), Belgium since 2023 (Royal Decree of 27 February 2023), and the Netherlands banned untargeted gambling ads in 2023 and is now closing the remaining loopholes.
- **No junk-food ads on the city's transport and property.** London did this in 2019 on Transport for London's advertising estate — one of the most valuable in the world — and a peer-reviewed evaluation found that household purchases of unhealthy food fell by roughly 1,000 calories per week. Amsterdam wrote the same exclusion into its metro advertising concession in 2018. The mechanism is a contract clause, not a law: Bucharest can adopt it the day an advertising contract comes up for renewal.

### Parks, lakes and wilderness are commons

- **Recover the 12 stolen hectares of IOR Park.** Twelve hectares of the park were retroceded in 2005 through a disposition the Prefect of Bucharest is now asking the courts to annul as abusive — a state restitution-agency report found signatures in the file that do not match, and the archives list the land as a park, which could not legally be returned in kind. Since then some 1,500 trees have been destroyed, the land has burned in more than 40 fires for which no one has been charged, and the €35 million fine issued by the city's own Local Police in 2023 remains uncollected. Past administrations lost this fight by not showing up: they submitted no evidence in court and let fines be annulled without appeal. We will litigate as if the park mattered, collect the fine, and — where courts fail — do what Sector 2 did at Plumbuita Park in February 2024, when it bought back four retroceded hectares for €4.3 million and returned them to the public domain.
- **A Green Space Register with legal force.** The register Bucharest is legally required to keep has been non-functional since 2013 — which is how the Environmental Guard could decline to protect the IOR hectares, whose cadastre entry says "buildable yards" rather than green space. Completing the register and anchoring every square meter of green space in urbanism documentation activates the strongest protection in Romanian law: land designated as green space cannot change destination, regardless of who owns it (OUG 195/2005, art. 71; Law 24/2007, art. 18). The next IOR must be impossible to reclassify out of existence.
- **Lakeshores you can reach without buying anything.** Brazil's coastal law declares every beach a public good of common use, with free access guaranteed in any direction (Lei 7.661/1988, art. 10) — which is why no sunbed operator there can wall off the shore. Spain's coastal law does the same (Ley de Costas, Ley 22/1988). Bucharest's lakes deserve the same principle. Press investigations have documented 44 contracts for lakeside terraces signed between 1997 and 2019 — the longest running to 2039, many built without permits inside the listed Herăstrău park. Audit and unwind them, and replace the enclosure model with the kiosk model: small, competitively tendered, revocable concessions that serve park visitors and pay rent to the public — and never block free access to the water. Commerce in a park is welcome as a service to the people in it, not as a gate around it.
- **More urban wilderness, not less.** Văcărești — 186 hectares of self-made wilderness declared a protected urban nature park in 2016 (HG 349/2016) after a civic campaign — proves that Bucharest wants wild places and knows how to defend them. Extend that model along the Colentina lake chain instead of concessioning it.

### A city you can move through

- **Public transport free at the point of use.** Free transport is often imagined as a luxury for rich cities; Bucharest's budget says otherwise. Fares cover only roughly 10–20% of the Bucharest system's costs — the rest is already public subsidy — and pupils and resident pensioners already ride free. Tallinn made public transport free for registered residents in 2013, and the city says the arithmetic paid for itself, because every newly registered resident brings income-tax revenue with them. Luxembourg went fare-free nationwide in 2020. Montpellier, a metropolitan area of half a million, went fare-free for residents in December 2023 and saw ridership rise by nearly a quarter within months. Phase it in — residents first, using the General Council's existing fare-setting mandate — while heeding Tallinn's lesson: free fares work when they ride on an expanding network, not instead of one.
- **Somewhere to put the bicycle.** The first blocker to cycling in Bucharest is not the missing lanes but the missing storage. Since 2017, every new French building with car parking must include secure bicycle parking (Code de la construction et de l'habitation, art. L113-18); every new Dutch dwelling must include a storage room of at least 5 m² reachable from the street (Bouwbesluit 2012); Berlin requires one to four bicycle spaces per apartment, by size (its 2021 parking rules). Bucharest is drafting a new General Urbanism Plan right now — write the same norms into its regulation. For the existing blocks: secure on-street bike hangars rented for a small fee, as London boroughs install them (Lambeth alone has over 800, with waiting lists in the tens of thousands) and as Rotterdam has done for decades with its *fietstrommels*.
- **Build the bike network the city already promised.** Bucharest has a Velo Masterplan targeting 565 km of routes by 2035 — and, by the last independent count, under 17 km of genuinely usable lanes, after €11 million was spent a decade ago painting sidewalks that the traffic police later closed. Paris shows what delivery looks like: after some €400 million across two *Plans Vélo*, bicycles overtook cars inside the city in 2024 — 11.2% of trips versus 4.3% by car.

### A home is a right

- **Build public housing again.** Romania holds two European records that explain each other: the EU's highest homeownership rate (around 95%) and its highest overcrowding rate (40.7% of the population, against an EU average of 17%). Social housing is 0.17% of the national stock — last in the EU — and Bucharest's General City Hall administers about 1,175 social dwellings, has built none since 2014, and allocated three in 2025. The legal machinery already exists: the Housing Law (Law 114/1996) lets the city build social housing and caps the tenant's rent at 10% of household income. What is missing is land, money and will.
- **Copy the city that never sold out: Vienna.** Around 60% of Vienna's residents live in municipal or limited-profit housing, where rents are cost-based by law (the WGG); the city's land fund, Wohnfonds Wien, has been buying land for public housing since 1984; and since 2019, two-thirds of the housing floor space on major rezoned land must be subsidized housing (the *geförderter Wohnbau* zoning category). That last rule is the one to copy first: Bucharest is rewriting its General Urbanism Plan, and every rezoning that showers a developer with land value can be made to pay for public homes.
- **Housing First for people on the street.** Finland gives homeless people a permanent home first and support services second; long-term homelessness fell by two-thirds between 2008 and 2022, and each person housed saves the public purse an estimated €9,600–15,000 a year. A city that already pays for emergency rooms and shelters can afford the cheaper policy that works.
- **Allocation by need, never by connections.** The city's more than 4,000 municipal dwellings were, until the scandal of 2026, rented at symbolic prices frozen since 2007 — to tenants who included the well-connected — while thousands of applicant families waited for years. Publish the tenant registers and waiting lists, audit every contract, and set the allocation criteria in public.
- **Make empty and derelict property pay.** Romanians pay under €100 per person per year in property tax, more than seven times below the EU average — yet at the 2021 census 21% of Bucharest's dwellings stood unoccupied, more than double the share of a decade earlier, in the same city that allocated three social homes in 2025. One lever is already in the city's hands: the Fiscal Code lets local authorities raise the tax on neglected buildings and land by up to 500% (Law 227/2015, art. 489), a tool Cluj-Napoca has used since 2016 to push the owners of over 800 decaying buildings to restore them — in Bucharest, only Sector 1 applies it in earnest. Apply it citywide. Taxing vacancy itself is beyond the city's powers — the Fiscal Code's list of local taxes is closed — so we militate nationally for the instrument our neighbours take for granted: Paris applies a 60% surcharge on second homes (Code général des impôts, art. 1407 ter), Ireland's Vacant Homes Tax now runs at seven times the base property tax, and research from the Barcelona Institute of Economics finds such taxes cut the number of empty homes by around 13%.

### Warm homes, clean energy

- **Stop heating the sky.** Bucharest runs one of the two largest district-heating systems in the European Union — by network length, the second in the world after Moscow — serving over a million people in half a million apartments. Roughly a third of the hot water pumped into it never reaches a home (the World Bank measured 28.6% of the heat lost in transit), because most of the network is past its design life. The subsidy that plugs this hole is the single largest expense in the city's budget: around 2.6 billion lei a year, more than half of all subsidies paid by Romania's 206 municipalities combined. The fix is not mysterious — replace 2–3% of the network every year, permanently. The pace nearly tripled between 2020 and 2024, from 27 to 73 km a year, and the money is largely European: €270 million in EU cohesion funds for 210 km of pipes, some €362 million from the Modernisation Fund for new cogeneration plants, a €300 million EIB framework loan. Sustain that pace across mandates, whoever the mayor is — and finish unifying production and distribution under the city, the transfer of ELCEN that the Government approved in May 2026 with a rationale we share: the split system is itself a cause of the losses.
- **A heat plan with a horizon.** Denmark's Heat Supply Act of 1979 made every municipality plan its heating decades ahead — the foundation of systems like Copenhagen's, where district heating reaches 98% of buildings and is close to carbon-neutral. EU law now asks the same of us: the Energy Efficiency Directive (EU) 2023/1791 requires every municipality above 45,000 inhabitants to draw up a local heating and cooling plan. Warsaw — a post-communist network of Bucharest's scale — proves such a system can simply work; Vienna and Munich are drilling for geothermal heat to make theirs carbon-neutral by 2040. Bucharest's heat plan should be drawn up in public and bind every mayor who follows.
- **Insulate the blocks — every sector, poorest first.** The national thermal-rehabilitation framework (OUG 18/2009) lets local councils cover the owners' share of insulation costs, and parts of Bucharest have used it at scale: Sector 1 has insulated 958 blocks at no cost to owners, around 90% of its eligible stock, and Sector 2 over 1,100 — while Sectors 5 and 6 lag far behind and Sector 6 charges owners 20–25%. Whether your home leaks heat should not depend on which side of a boulevard you live on. A citywide program should coordinate and co-finance the laggards, taking the worst-performing blocks and the poorest households first — exactly the order the EU's recast buildings directive (EU/2024/1275) now demands — in a country where one person in ten cannot keep their home adequately warm (Eurostat, 2024).
- **Energy communities on the city's roofs.** After years in which Romania's transposition of the EU rules (OUG 163/2022) copied the definitions without the machinery, the 2025–2026 amendments and ANRE's new national register finally make energy communities operational — and Cooperativa de Energie, founded in 2018 and now approaching a thousand members, already supplies citizen-owned green power. Cities elsewhere show what a municipality can add: since 2012 Vienna's utility has sold citizens shares in solar plants it builds — the first sold out in 26 hours, and some 15,000 Viennese have invested over €37 million — a design made precisely for people who rent and own no roof; in Paris, the cooperative EnerCit'IF installs citizen-financed solar on school roofs; Barcelona created a fully public electricity supplier, Barcelona Energia, with solar on more than 130 municipal buildings. In Bucharest the sectors have begun — Sector 3 is covering its schools with some 13,000 panels — but no citywide program exists. Open the city's roofs to citizen energy cooperatives, and make the municipality a founding member of Bucharest's first energy communities.

### Culture that opens its doors

- **Free Sundays at the city's museums.** The municipality owns a network of twelve museums and memorial houses — from Suțu Palace to the Pinacoteca and the Urseanu Observatory — where a full ticket costs 20 lei and no regular free day is on the official calendar, even as several state museums in the city already open free one day a month. Italy has opened its state museums free on the first Sunday of every month since 2014 (*Domenica al Museo*, over 480 sites today): in its first year attendance rose and paid ticket revenue rose with it, because free days create museum-goers. Britain went further — its national museums have been free every day since 2001, and visits to the formerly charging ones rose by about 150% over the following decade. One free Sunday a month across every museum the city owns is a decision the General Council can take with a single vote.

### A city its residents govern

- **Participatory budgeting, at last.** A fixed share of the city's investment budget proposed and voted directly by residents. Paris has put around 5% of its investment budget — on the order of €100 million a year — into its residents' hands since 2014; Lisbon became the first European capital to do it in 2008; and Cluj-Napoca has run participatory budgeting since 2017, so the precedent is not just European but Romanian. Bucharest's investment budget for 2025 was about 2.35 billion lei: the same 5% would mean over 100 million lei a year of projects chosen by the people who live with the results. Run it on Decidim, the open-source participation platform built by Barcelona and used by cities across Europe — the infrastructure of democracy should be owned by no one.
- **Neighbourhood councils.** Standing councils with a consultative role and a direct channel into city hall, on the model of France's *conseils de quartier*, mandatory in larger French cities since the 2002 *loi relative à la démocratie de proximité*. In Bucharest they have an extra job: the neighbourhood is where the gap between the General City Hall and the six sectors — the gap every failing service falls into — is actually felt, and residents deserve one table at which both must answer.
- **A permanent citizens' assembly.** In October 2021 Paris created a standing assembly of one hundred residents drawn by lot — a statistical portrait of the city — that deliberates year-round and submits its deliberations to the city council. For the questions Bucharest's politicians keep dodging, from retroceded parks to the fate of the lakeshores, let a random sample of the city hear the evidence and speak.
- **A glass city hall.** Every scandal in this document is also an opacity scandal: lakeside contracts nobody could read, municipal tenant lists nobody could see, a green-space register nobody kept. Publish every contract, concession, subsidy and line of spending of the municipality and its companies as open, searchable data.

## How we pay for it

This program is cheaper than it looks, because the status quo is far more expensive than it looks. Bucharest already pays, every year, for heat that escapes into the ground, rents that were never collected, fines that were never enforced and land that was given away. Most of this program is not new spending; it is the end of old bleeding.

- **Plug the biggest leak.** The heating subsidy — 2.6 billion lei a year for a network that loses a third of its heat — is the largest line in the city's budget. Every kilometer of pipe replaced shrinks it permanently.
- **Collect what is owed.** Correcting the symbolic rents on the city's municipal dwellings is worth around 500 million lei a year by the mayor's own estimate; the uncollected IOR fine is €35 million; the lakeside concessions pay a pittance for prime public waterfront. For comparison, making all surface transport free costs about 215 million lei a year in forgone fares — the uncollected rents alone would pay for it twice over.
- **Tax idleness, not work.** The derelict-buildings surcharge citywide and top-of-range rates on large commercial property are the city's to set today; the vacancy tax — and redirecting the existing national 0.9% luxury-property tax to the cities where the properties stand — is what we demand from Parliament.
- **Build with European money.** For heating alone, €270 million in cohesion funds, some €362 million from the Modernisation Fund and a €300 million EIB loan are already approved. Bucharest's constraint has never been the availability of EU money; it has been the will to absorb it.
- **Much of the rest is free, or pays for itself.** The advertising regulation costs nothing; the Vienna zoning rule makes rezoning windfalls fund the public homes; Housing First saves more than it costs; participatory budgeting reallocates money rather than adding any; Italy's free museum Sundays raised revenue. The one honest exception is building social housing at scale, which needs initial credit — repaid over decades by cost rents, as Vienna has proven for a century.

Almost everything here is already within the city's powers; where it is not, we say so and name who must act. All of it asks for the same thing: a city hall that remembers whom Bucharest belongs to.
